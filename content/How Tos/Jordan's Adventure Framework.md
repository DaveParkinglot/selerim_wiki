# Introductory Notes
- This is meant to be a list of suggestions to help you create your very own D&D adventure. 
- Each section will provide examples from an adventure I already ran ([[The Crimsonclaw Bounty|The Crimsonclaw Bounty]]), and also may include a subsection of pointers for DMing in Foundry VTT.
- Each section has a recommendation on how much time you should spend on it. I recommend sessions last no more than 3 hours, and you should also keep room for two 5 minute breaks.
- For your adventure, you might want to swap some of these parts around—your adventure doesn’t need to be ordered the same way that my framework is.
- This is NOT a Foundry VTT tutorial, you’ll have to get that from somewhere else.
---

> [!note] Parts 0-4 below provide a list of the things you need to do to get your adventure up and running (Parts 0-4). I’ve also added some helpful stuff at the end, but you don’t need that to make your adventure.

# Part 0: The Premise & Setup
The “hook” or the premise of the adventure should be a short paragraph, or a list of ideas to grab players’ imaginations while giving some high-level background on what their characters will be doing in the adventure.
___
**Example Premise from The Crimsonclaw Bounty:**
- For a sleepy fishing village, Crabbley has always had more than its fair share of threats from both the land and the sea. 
- The latest monster to terrorize the town is a manticore the locals call "Old Crimsonclaw". 
- After a string of gruesome attacks on townsfolk and livestock alike, Mayor Bastifor has posted a 450 gold bounty on Old Crimsonclaw's head.
- Your adventure begins at the Crab Claw Inn, a venue well known for cold brews and surf and turf specials. Whoever your character is, whether brave or foolish, they will soon leave the safety of the town to track Old Crimsonclaw through the twisting trails of the Tanglewood wilds…
---

# Part 1: Introduction - 15-30 Minutes
This is the most free form part of the adventure, so you’ll just have to use your creativity and your best judgement. The players might start in a tavern, on the road, or even in the middle of a high stakes poker game. No matter where your adventure begins, you should aim to accomplish these 2 things here:

- Hook introduction. Why should the player characters care?
- Player character introductions. Who are these people?

 I recommend having each player roll on a random relationship table to encourage some worldbuilding and roleplaying. In Foundry, I’ve built a macro into each character sheet in the Biography tab to make this easy. 

--- 
Example Introduction notes from The Crimsonclaw Bounty:
- Characters meet up at the Crab Claw Inn in Crabbley
- The notice board has a bounty posted on the manticore Old Crimsonclaw, with a 450 gold reward for the first adventuring party to bring Old Crimsonclaw’s head to Mayor Bastifor
---
**Foundry Tips:**
*“You begin in a tavern…”* is overplayed for a reason (because it works!). I have many pre-built tavern scenes for notable taverns all over the world of Selerim. Each tavern is labeled with its name and the city/town it is in.
- In Foundry, you can find these pre-built tavern scenes here: Scenes > Taverns of Selerim

# Part 2: Challenge Montage - 30-45 Minutes
A challenge montage allows players to feel out how their characters work and interact with the rest of the party, while also expending some of their resources (HP, Inspiration, spells, etc.). I usually run this early in the session, often after the Introduction. Your challenge montage should be able to clearly answer the following questions:
- What obstacle(s) are the characters trying to overcome?
- What are the consequences of failure, or the rewards that come with success?

You can find many different ways to run these types of challenges online. My preferred method is to use modified Skill Challenge rules from D&D 4e and a Travel Consequences table full of mostly negative effects. 
Here is how I usually run these challenges:
- In initiative order, players take turns describing what their characters do to overcome the obstacle. 
- The DM decides what kind of check makes sense given the context of the challenge. 
	- Usually this is a skill check, but at your discretion, players can also choose to use their equipment or even spells.
- Players roll against a Difficulty Class (DC) decided by the DM. This DC may change as the rounds progress, depending on the skill challenge.
- At the end of each round, the DM determines outcomes by rolling on a Travel Outcomes table for each failure in that round. You can use a standard Travel Outcomes table, but I usually make one that is custom for the adventure.

I like to think of this part of the session as a montage, with each round representing some amount of time, usually an hour.

---
**Example Challenge Montage setup from The Crimsonclaw Bounty:**
- The characters must navigate the Tanglewood wilds to locate the Greenspring Trail, the closest known trail to Old Crimsonclaw’s lair.
- Each round represents ~2 hours of travel.
- Roll travel outcomes from the “Tanglewood Travel - Crimsonclaw” table, a custom table I created for this adventure.
---
**Foundry Tips:**
- There are many existing resources available in Foundry to help you create your own skill challenge.
- In Foundry, rules for skill challenges can be found here: Journal Entries > Rules and Notes > Rules > House Rule: Challenge Montage
- In Foundry, Travel Outcomes tables can be found here: Rollable Tables > Travel Outcomes

# Part 3: Optional Encounter - 30-45 minutes
I like to put in one encounter that the characters can bypass or expedite with either stealth, clever roleplay, or by some other means. This gives you some flexibility as a DM if things are moving slower or faster than you expected. I have no specific advice for how to design this encounter, but here are some guidelines:
- The encounter should fit the theme of your adventure. Maybe drop some loot here for the players. Or try foreshadowing, it’s a good trick.
- The encounter should be flexible, but as you see in the example scene notes, I try to put some Difficulty Class (DC) guidelines for the scene. I don’t always do this though, so  don’t overthink it and figure out what works best for you.

While this ISN’T a guide on how to create fun & balanced encounters, here are a few resources that can help you with encounter building:
- Read the Dungeon Master’s Guide (DMG). This will make you a better DM and a better person. Free Online DMG: [https://5e.tools/book.html#xdmg](https://5e.tools/book.html#xdmg)
- Free online encounter builder: [https://redcap.press/encounters](https://redcap.press/encounters)
---
**Example Scene Notes from The Crimsonclaw Bounty:**
- A rarely traveled, overgrown trail with a few old elvish ruins to the south of the beaten path between Crabbley and its sister town of Crillville
- If the players accumulate too many failures in the Skill Challenge, they might arrive at night. This will make visibility lower and Jiggy Mudpie the Mud Mephit might not be there to warn them about the crocodile ambush
- Leonine footprints can be followed to a dead member of the Crillville Defense Force (DC 12 perception). The footprints disappear on the north side of the alcove indicating that the creature may have flown away (DC 12 Investigation)
- Jiggy Mudpie the Mud Mephit plays in the mud just before the Greenspring hot spring that gives the trail its name. If the party dares step in his mud they will be hostile, but they prefer to avoid conflict generally
- If the party is nice enough (or rolls a DC 14 Charisma check) they might tell them about the crocodiles lying in ambush in the muddy stream. Otherwise, Crocodiles surprise em

# Part 4: The Final Encounter - 45-60 minutes
The final encounter of your adventure should almost always resolve as a combat encounter. By this point, players should be well acquainted with their character sheets, so give them a challenge. Again, this is not a guide on encounter design, but here are some basic pointers:
- Don’t put a million enemies in this encounter. Keep it to the boss and maybe just a few henchmen. You won’t want to keep track of that many things in combat, trust me. Ideally, your turn as the DM should take no longer than 2 minutes.
- If you want to give out loot or plant some hooks for future adventures, this is where you should do that.
---
**Example Scene Notes from The Crimsonclaw Bounty:**
- Looks like a story you wouldn't believe if you heard it, but the Leaky Bucket, famed ship of privateer Captain Winchester Bone, is suspended in vines in the jungle canopy (history check?) 
- Old Crimsonclaw's nest is atop the old ship 
- In the captain's quarters there is a chest of loot 
- Have to climb the ship (1/2 movement), or you can hide in the jungle thickets to avoid being hit by Crimsonclaw's spine attacks
- Crimsonclaw has a tag on his ear with notes from his creator
---
**Foundry Tips:**
- Curating loot for the party or creating your own items can be fun and rewarding. But if you’d rather leave it to chance, I have created tons of random loot items in Foundry.  
- In Foundry, find these random loot roll items here: Items > Loot Rolls
- Just don’t be the guy who gives a Legendary item to a level 3 party. Consult the [Magic Items section of the DMG](https://5e.tools/book.html#xdmg,6,magic%20items) if you want guidelines on loot distribution.
